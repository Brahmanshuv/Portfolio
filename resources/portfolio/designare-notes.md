# Designare — Build a Portfolio Which Actually Makes Money
## Structured Notes & Portfolio Review Framework

**Source:** Designare — *Build a portfolio which actually makes money!*  
**Video length:** ~65:46  
**Use case:** Portfolio strategy, positioning, homepage review, case-study editing, and hiring-focused product design presentation.

---

# 1. Core Idea

> **A portfolio is not a gallery. It is a decision-making tool.**

The purpose of a portfolio is not simply to show beautiful work.

Its purpose is to help the right viewer make a decision:

- Understand who you are
- Understand what kind of problems you solve
- Trust that you can solve them
- Want you specifically
- Know what to do next

The portfolio should make the viewer think:

> **“I want this person working on my problem.”**

A strong portfolio therefore creates four things:

1. **Clarity**
2. **Trust**
3. **Desire**
4. **Action**

---

# 2. Positioning > Pure Visual Quality

Two designers may have:

- Similar skill
- Similar years of experience
- Similar visual quality
- Similar process

Yet one can command much better opportunities.

The difference is often **positioning**.

A generic designer says:

> “I am a UI/UX designer and I create beautiful digital experiences.”

A positioned designer communicates:

- Who they help
- What kinds of problems they solve
- What environment they are strong in
- What outcomes they care about
- What kind of work they want next

The portfolio should answer:

> **Why are YOU the right person for THIS kind of problem?**

---

# 3. Portfolio Value Is Perceived Value

A portfolio influences perceived professional value.

The viewer is not only evaluating:

- “Can this person use Figma?”
- “Can this person make attractive UI?”

They are evaluating:

- Can they understand difficult problems?
- Can they work within constraints?
- Can they make useful decisions?
- Can they communicate clearly?
- Can they work at the level I need?
- Will they reduce risk for my team?
- Can I trust them with an important product?

This is why a strategically structured portfolio can dramatically outperform a visually impressive but generic one.

---

# 4. The Four Jobs of a Portfolio

## 4.1 Clarity

The visitor should quickly understand:

- Who are you?
- What do you do?
- What type of work do you specialize in?
- What kind of problems can you solve?

Avoid vague portfolio language.

Weak:

> Product Designer creating meaningful digital experiences.

Better:

> Product Designer focused on complex SaaS workflows, dashboards, and scalable product systems.

The exact wording depends on the target role.

---

## 4.2 Trust

Trust comes from evidence.

Examples:

- Real product work
- Recognizable companies
- Outcomes
- Testimonials
- Strong project depth
- Measurable improvements
- Complex problems
- Strong decision-making
- Consistency across the portfolio

Trust is not created merely by adding:

- More gradients
- More animations
- More screenshots
- More sections

---

## 4.3 Desire

The viewer should move from:

> “This designer seems capable.”

to:

> **“I want THIS designer.”**

This comes from:

- Clear specialization
- Strong taste
- Strong point of view
- Relevant projects
- Confident storytelling
- Demonstrated thinking
- Memorable visual identity

---

## 4.4 Action

The visitor should know what to do next.

Examples:

- Contact
- Book a call
- Email
- View resume
- View LinkedIn
- Continue to another project

Do not make the visitor hunt for the next action.

---

# 5. The Hero Section

## The hero has ONE job:

> **Make the right person keep scrolling.**

The hero should establish enough information above the fold to answer:

### Identity
Who are you?

### Positioning
What kind of designer are you?

### Proof
Why should I believe you?

### Curiosity
Why should I keep looking?

A visually impressive hero that fails to communicate positioning is still weak.

---

# 6. Above-the-Fold Checklist

Within the first screen, try to establish:

- Name / identity
- Product design role
- Specific design positioning
- One strong credibility signal
- Current status or availability when relevant
- Visual personality
- A reason to continue scrolling

The viewer should not need 30 seconds to determine what kind of designer they are looking at.

---

# 7. Portfolio Project Order Is Positioning

> **Project order communicates what you want to be hired for.**

Do not automatically sort work chronologically.

Instead ask:

> Which project best represents the type of work I want next?

Put that project first.

If targeting:

- SaaS product design
- Enterprise products
- B2B systems
- Product systems
- Dashboard-heavy roles

then the portfolio should lead with the project that proves those capabilities most strongly.

Your first project becomes your strongest positioning signal.

---

# 8. Quality Over Quantity

A strong principle from the video:

> **2–3 directionally strong projects are better than 10–12 mediocre ones.**

A project should exist because it strengthens your positioning.

Do not include a project simply because:

- You worked on it
- It is old work
- You spent a lot of time on it
- You need to fill space

Every project should contribute to the argument you are making about yourself.

---

# 9. Case Studies Are Not Documentation

One of the most important principles:

> **A case study should show how you think — not everything you did.**

Weak case study:

- Research screenshot
- Sticky notes
- Personas
- Journey map
- Wireframes
- UI
- Prototype

This may show activity, but not necessarily judgment.

Strong case study:

- What was happening?
- What was difficult?
- What decision had to be made?
- What information influenced the decision?
- What alternatives existed?
- Why was one direction selected?
- What was executed?
- What happened afterward?

---

# 10. Recommended Case Study Structure

Use:

## 1. Context
What is the product?

Who is it for?

What environment does it operate in?

What were the constraints?

---

## 2. Problem
What specifically was broken, difficult, unclear, slow, inefficient, risky, or valuable?

Do not make the problem excessively broad.

---

## 3. Decision
This is often the most important part.

Explain:

- What decision did you make?
- Why?
- What evidence influenced it?
- What alternatives did you reject?
- What trade-offs existed?
- What constraints shaped the solution?

This is where seniority becomes visible.

---

## 4. Execution
Show the UI, interactions, system, prototype, architecture, flows, visual design, etc.

Execution should support the decision — not replace it.

---

## 5. Outcome
Show:

- Business outcome
- Product outcome
- User outcome
- Process improvement
- Learning
- Validation
- Adoption
- Impact
- Next steps

Use metrics when they genuinely exist.

Do not invent numbers.

---

# 11. Decisions Are the Most Valuable Signal

Screenshots show what something looks like.

Decisions reveal how the designer works.

Hiring managers care about:

- Judgment
- Trade-offs
- Prioritization
- Constraints
- Product thinking
- Collaboration
- Problem framing
- Systems thinking

Therefore case studies should emphasize key decision points.

---

# 12. Stop Documenting. Start Editing.

> **Stop documenting. Start editing.**

Do not include every artifact created during the project.

The portfolio is not a project archive.

Remove:

- Repetitive screens
- Redundant research screenshots
- Decorative diagrams without meaning
- Generic UX process templates
- Screens that do not reinforce the story
- Information the viewer does not need

Keep only what strengthens the central argument.

---

# 13. Every Section Must Have a Business Job

Ask of every section:

> **Why does this section exist?**

A section should ideally improve at least one of:

- Clarity
- Trust
- Desire
- Action

If it does none of these, consider removing it.

Examples:

### Experience
**Job:** Build trust.

### Selected Work
**Job:** Prove capabilities.

### Testimonials
**Job:** Reduce perceived risk.

### Skills
**Job:** Clarify technical/tool competence.

### About
**Job:** Humanize the designer and strengthen fit.

### Contact
**Job:** Create action.

---

# 14. Better Positioning Often Comes From Removing

> **Better positioning often comes from removing.**

More work ≠ stronger portfolio.

More sections ≠ stronger portfolio.

More words ≠ stronger story.

More animations ≠ more value.

Strong portfolios deliberately exclude things that dilute the desired perception.

Ask:

> What do I NOT want the viewer to associate me with?

Remove work that sends the wrong signal.

---

# 15. Your Portfolio Is an Argument

A portfolio should make a specific argument.

Example:

> Brahmanshu is a product designer who can understand complex digital products, simplify difficult workflows, create scalable systems, and produce highly polished interfaces.

Every section should provide evidence for that claim.

If one project contradicts the desired positioning, it can weaken the entire portfolio.

---

# 16. Your Portfolio Should Predict Your Future

A powerful principle:

> **Your portfolio should predict the kind of work you want next.**

Do not treat the portfolio purely as a historical archive.

If you want:

- SaaS projects → show SaaS
- Enterprise workflows → show enterprise complexity
- AI products → show AI product thinking
- Fintech → show fintech-style problems
- Premium consumer products → show polished consumer work

The portfolio should visually demonstrate your future direction.

---

# 17. “Want It? Show It.”

If you want a particular kind of work, visually prove that you can do it.

Do not expect the viewer to imagine your potential.

Examples:

Want enterprise work?

Show:

- Data-heavy interfaces
- Permission systems
- Complex workflows
- Information architecture
- Multi-role systems

Want premium consumer product work?

Show:

- Strong motion
- Tasteful visual systems
- Highly polished UI
- Microinteractions
- Strong brand/product integration

---

# 18. What If You Do Not Have Enough Client Work?

Proof does not need to come only from paid client work.

Possible sources:

- Self-initiated projects
- Real-world design briefs
- Concept redesigns
- Founder collaborations
- Volunteer work
- Micro case studies
- Product teardowns
- Design explorations
- System redesigns

The important thing is that the project proves relevant capability.

---

# 19. Do Not Fake Complexity

A conceptual project still needs:

- Real constraints
- Logical decisions
- Clear reasoning
- Realistic user needs
- Realistic product structure

Avoid:

> “I redesigned Spotify because I didn't like the UI.”

Prefer:

> “I explored how Spotify could improve discovery for X user segment under Y constraint.”

---

# 20. Website / Business Communication Framework

A useful structure shown in the video:

### WHAT
What do you offer?

### WHO
Who is it for?

### WHY YOU
Why should someone choose you?

### PROOF
Show evidence.

### BUY / ACT
Make the next action obvious.

For a designer portfolio, adapt this to:

### WHAT
What kind of designer are you?

### WHO / PROBLEM
What product environments or problems are you good at?

### WHY YOU
What differentiates your approach?

### PROOF
Case studies, experience, outcomes.

### ACTION
Contact / hire / interview.

---

# 21. Portfolio Review Question

Instead of asking:

> “How do I make my portfolio cooler?”

Ask:

> **“What do I want this portfolio to make people believe?”**

Then design the site around that belief.

---

# 22. Positioning Formula

Positioning can be shaped through:

### Who you help
Example:
SaaS teams, startups, enterprise products, consumer products.

### What you solve
Example:
Complex workflows, onboarding, information architecture, dashboards, design systems.

### What you show
Your projects and proof.

### What you talk about
Case-study narrative and emphasis.

### What you deliberately do NOT show
Anything that weakens your positioning.

---

# 23. Portfolio Design vs Portfolio Strategy

Visual design still matters.

Strong execution creates:

- Memorability
- Perceived quality
- Taste
- Credibility
- Emotional desire

But visual design must support strategy.

A beautiful portfolio with unclear positioning may still underperform.

The goal is:

> **Strategy + Proof + Taste**

—not visuals alone.

---

# 24. Motion and Visual Presentation

Motion should reinforce:

- Product interaction
- Hierarchy
- Usability
- System behavior
- Storytelling
- Delight

Avoid animation purely because animation is impressive.

Large visuals should be used when they help communicate:

- Product behavior
- System complexity
- Interaction quality
- Before/after differences
- Key workflows

# 29. How to Use Large Visuals

Full-width project pages are useful when the content deserves the area.

Use large visual blocks for:

- Hero product footage
- Interaction videos
- Important workflows
- Before/after comparison
- System architecture
- High-fidelity final interfaces
- Key product moments

Avoid using huge blocks simply because unused horizontal space exists.

The visual should always have a storytelling function.

---

# 30. Sticky Project Navigation

For long case studies, persistent navigation can improve usability.

Possible sections:

- Overview
- Problem
- Strategy
- Decisions
- System
- Solution
- Outcome

Sticky project navigation is particularly useful when a page is highly visual and vertically long.

---

# 31. What to Remove From Case Studies

Potential deletion candidates:

- Generic persona templates
- Excessive sticky-note screenshots
- Giant blocks of process documentation
- Repetitive device mockups
- Multiple screens proving the same point
- Decorative diagrams
- Long textbook UX explanations
- Unnecessary UI shots
- Fake metrics
- Generic “Design Process” graphics

---

# 32. What to Add Instead

Prioritize:

- Decision explanations
- Trade-offs
- Constraints
- Before/after comparisons
- User/system flow
- System architecture
- Product videos
- Interactive prototypes
- Measurable outcomes
- Short captions explaining why the screen matters

---

# 33. Senior Designer Signal

A senior portfolio communicates:

> “Here is why I made this decision.”

not simply:

> “Here is the UI I made.”

The viewer should be able to infer:

- Strategic thinking
- Product thinking
- Systems thinking
- Visual craft
- Communication
- Prioritization
- Ownership

---

# 34. Homepage Audit Framework

For every homepage section ask:

### Clarity
Does this explain who Brahmanshu is or what he does?

### Trust
Does this make his capability more believable?

### Desire
Does this make the viewer more interested in working with him?

### Action
Does this help the viewer take the next step?

If a section scores zero across all four, delete it.

---

# 35. Project Audit Framework

For each project ask:

1. What capability does this project prove?
2. What target role does it support?
3. What is the central problem?
4. What were the most important decisions?
5. Are those decisions visible?
6. Is there too much documentation?
7. Are screenshots being used as evidence or decoration?
8. Is the project differentiated from the other projects?
9. Does the outcome feel credible?
10. Would a hiring manager remember the project tomorrow?

---

# 36. 8-Second Test

Within roughly the first few seconds, a visitor should understand:

- Who you are
- What you do
- What type of product designer you are
- Whether the work looks relevant
- Whether they want to continue

The site should not require interpretation.

# 37. Recruiter vs Design Manager

Different viewers scan differently.

## Recruiter
Likely cares quickly about:

- Role
- Experience
- Companies
- Location
- Resume
- Skills
- Project quality

## Design Manager
Likely cares more about:

- Decision-making
- Product thinking
- Complexity
- Process judgment
- Craft
- System thinking
- Ownership
- Outcomes

The portfolio should serve both.

## End Note

The most important shift from the Designare video is moving from **portfolio decoration** to **portfolio strategy**.

Great visual design remains important, but every visual, section, case study, sentence, motion treatment, and project should contribute to a deliberate perception.

The portfolio should be edited like a product:

- Understand the audience
- Define the desired outcome
- Remove friction
- Prioritize important information
- Establish trust
- Demonstrate value
- Create a clear next action
