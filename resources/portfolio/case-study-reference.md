# Brahmanshu Verma — Portfolio Project Page Playbook

Prepared 1 September 2026  
For: Dream Holidays, Cora, Staple, and 6s SEO Tool

## The decision in one paragraph

The four project pages should share one calm editorial system, but they should not tell the same story four times. Each page needs a specific tension, a human opening, a quick evidence-backed summary, three to five decision-led chapters, and a confident ending. The pages should be enjoyable because their pacing, section titles, and visuals carry the reader—not because everything moves or every sentence tries to be clever. Every image or graphic has a named job. Until the real asset is connected, the page should display a polished, accessible placeholder with that job written inside it.

Use this document as the next Antigravity implementation brief. The project chapters contain proposed reader-facing copy; lines beginning **Editor — not public** and **Insert** are production instructions, never website text. Match each draft to the original project material before publishing it.

### The synthesis

Indeed supplies the dependable narrative spine: one problem, one angle, a response, and an honest conclusion. Wall of Portfolios adds the hiring layer: role, constraints, key decisions, real product evidence, and a title that creates curiosity. The page system below combines both.

## One shared editorial shell

### Reading order

| Beat | What the reader should learn | Length target | Visual role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Hook | What became clearer, easier, or more coherent? | 8–14 words | One strong real-product hero or intentional placeholder |
| 2. Quick brief | Product, status, role, timeframe if verified, contribution, and outcome/status | 70–110 words | Metadata strip; no tool list unless it matters |
| 3. Stakes | Who is trying to do what, and what gets in the way? | 100–160 words | Current-state screen, workflow, or artifact |
| 4. Turning point | What did the designer realise or choose? | 80–140 words | Comparison, decision graphic, or annotated screen |
| 5. Product response | How does the flow or system work? | 180–320 words across short chapters | Real screens, sequence, prototype, and system details |
| 6. Resilience | What happens beyond the easiest path? | 60–120 words | Only evidenced states; proposals labelled separately |
| 7. Outcome | What is genuinely known? | 60–120 words | Verified result, qualitative evidence, or measurement plan |
| 8. Reflection | What would the designer improve or test next? | 50–90 words | Small quiet closing visual, not another fake metric |

### Page behaviour

- Keep the article body around 680–760px wide and project media up to about 1180–1240px wide.
- Let the hero and one or two key flows break wider than the text column. Do not make every section full-bleed.
- Keep a compact sticky contents rail on large screens. Collapse it into an accessible “On this page” control on narrow screens.
- Use project-specific accents sparingly: Cora can feel analytical, Staple tactile/structured, Dream Holidays warm/editorial, and 6s precise/data-led.
- Preserve Brahmanshu's premium dark shell and restrained frost. Screenshots may sit on light neutral canvases when their native UI needs it.
- Use one dominant idea per viewport. A large sentence, diagram, or screen may own a section; three should not compete at once.
- Motion should explain reveal or continuity: 160–240ms section/figure transitions, no scroll hijacking, no text that waits to become readable, and full reduced-motion support.
- Render project copy as semantic HTML. Figures require a caption; real images require useful alt text; decorative graphics require empty alt text.
- Do not globally shrink laptop layouts to solve 2K scaling. Verify phone, 1440px laptop, 1920px desktop, and 2560px 2K separately.

## How to make it fun without making it unserious

- Use one memorable line at the start of each chapter.
- Follow the line with plain language and evidence.
- Vary sentence length. A short line can create pace. Then let the explanation breathe.
- Prefer questions that expose the design problem: “Where should this page go next?” is stronger than “Our user-centric challenge.”
- Use small bits of humanity—“Five actions. One workspace.”—but avoid stand-up-comedy captions or emoji-heavy copy.
- Name chapters by the decision or tension, not the standard design-process stage.
- Replace “Research,” “Ideation,” and “Final UI” with the actual event: “The report had data, but no next move,” “A travel companion, not a checkout,” or “Preview before commitment.”
- Never fabricate a customer quote for personality. The writer's voice can provide personality on its own.

## Purposeful media placeholder system

A missing asset should never become a broken `<img>`, a fake UI screenshot, or a vague grey rectangle. Store it as a media record with `src: null`. Render an art-directed placeholder containing:

- stable asset ID;
- image, diagram, sequence, or video type;
- exact subject to insert;
- desired ratio;
- optional annotation direction;
- draft caption;
- evidence class: original design, explanatory graphic, or proposed/future state.

Suggested data shape:

```js
{
  id: 'COR-04',
  kind: 'image',
  src: null,
  poster: null,
  aspectRatio: '4 / 3',
  title: 'Decision detail and rationale',
  assetBrief: 'Use the supplied Cora screen that shows a recommendation beside the context required to review it.',
  annotationBrief: 'Add no more than three callouts: trigger, supporting context, human action.',
  captionDraft: 'The interface keeps the recommendation, its context, and the final human decision in one reading path.',
  alt: null,
  evidenceType: 'original-design',
  status: 'missing'
}
```

The placeholder should be useful in the local preview: a quiet line-art cue based on the asset type, the ID, and the brief. It must not mimic a finished dashboard. Once `src` is present, the same component becomes a real `<figure>` without changing layout.

There are 32 planned media records, not a requirement to fabricate 32 artifacts. Each record can be `ready`, `missing`, or `not-applicable`. A genuinely unavailable asset remains a purposeful placeholder. A feature or alternative that was never designed is `not-applicable` and omitted from the rendered story, with the reason retained in the manifest. A caption draft becomes a factual caption only after it matches the connected asset.

## Project 1 — Cora

### Story angle

**Project label:** Cora — Retail Media Network (RMN)  
**Proposed headline:** Campaign decisions, with the context still attached.  
**Proposed deck:** A product-design case study about organising retail-media information so teams can understand a campaign, inspect what matters, and stay in control of the next step.

This angle is intentionally about decision clarity. It does not claim that Cora shipped, improved revenue, or automated a workflow unless the original material proves those things.

### Proposed page narrative

#### 01 — A dashboard can be busy and still leave one question unanswered

Campaign work produces plenty to look at. The harder job is knowing what deserves attention and what should happen next. Cora explores how a Retail Media Network workspace can carry a reader from the campaign overview into the evidence behind a decision—without dropping the context along the way.

My role was Product Designer. The work focuses on hierarchy, progressive detail, decision support, and the points where a person should remain in control.

**Insert `COR-01` immediately after this opening.**

#### 02 — See the situation before opening another panel

The first layer should create orientation. What campaign is this? What state is it in? Which signals deserve a closer look? The overview earns its place only when it makes the next inspection easier. The design question is not how much information fits on the screen, but which information earns the first look.

**Editor — not public:** Use the original overview and annotate no more than three hierarchy decisions. Remove any claim the screen cannot support.

**Insert `COR-02` and `COR-03`.**

#### 03 — The recommendation is only half the interface

An assisted recommendation creates a second design problem: how does someone decide whether to trust it? The explanation matters as much as the suggestion. The design needs to make three things legible: what triggered the recommendation, what supports it, and what the person can do next.

**Editor — not public:** Include this chapter only if the source contains assisted recommendations. Otherwise use a context/detail chapter. Unimplemented behaviour belongs under a clearly labelled future test.

> A confident interface does not ask for blind confidence.

**Insert `COR-04` and `COR-05`.**

#### 04 — Keep movement visible

The design is easiest to understand in motion: an overview becomes a closer inspection, an inspection informs a decision, and the user returns with their place intact. A short interaction can explain that continuity more clearly than another page of screens.

**Editor — not public:** Select the strongest original clip that actually demonstrates this progression. Adjust the copy to the clip. Do not use all seven videos merely because they exist.

**Insert `COR-06`.**

#### 05 — Design the boundary, not just the happy path

The useful edge of a decision-support tool appears when information is incomplete or a suggestion does not fit. What does the interface explain? What can the person change? Where do they recover? Those questions define the next layer of the work.

**Editor — not public:** Show only source-backed states as completed designs. Place unanswered questions under “What I would test next.” Do not invent rollback or versioning.

**Insert `COR-07`.**

#### 06 — What this work demonstrates

Cora brings the design problem back to a practical goal: help someone understand a situation well enough to make the next call. The artifact can show hierarchy, continuity, and control. The next evaluation should test whether those choices support decision-making in practice.

**Editor — not public:** Treat the evaluation as proposed. Add actual findings only if a real evaluation exists.

**Insert `COR-08`, then the next-project handoff.**

## Project 2 — Staple

### Story angle

**Project label:** Staple — Document Editor  
**Proposed headline:** Give every page a place to go.  
**Proposed deck:** A four-day document-workspace assignment exploring how finance and operations users can split, merge, reorganise, preview, and export pages without losing confidence in the result.

### Proposed page narrative

#### 01 — Five actions. One workspace.

Document operations sound simple when listed as verbs: split, merge, reorganise, preview, export. The complexity appears when they have to work together. A person needs to know what is selected, where each page belongs, what will change, and whether it is safe to continue.

The brief was to design that workspace. The response focused on a coherent mental model rather than five disconnected tools.

**Insert `STP-01`.**

#### 02 — The object is a page, but the context is a document

A page can be moved independently, yet its source and destination still matter. The interface therefore needs to show page identity and document structure at the same time. That relationship is the foundation of the workspace: a page should be movable without becoming anonymous.

**Editor — not public:** Pair the editor overview with a simple graphic of visible object relationships. Do not invent hidden system behaviour.

**Insert `STP-02` and `STP-03`.**

#### 03 — Selection should feel obvious before movement feels fast

Bulk actions become risky when selection is ambiguous. Before making an action faster, the interface needs to make its target unmistakable: what is selected, what the action affects, and what stays untouched. That is the foundation for a workspace people can manipulate with confidence.

**Editor — not public:** Demonstrate only selection levels present in the source. Put missing levels in a future-state note.

> Speed is useful. Confidence is non-negotiable.

**Insert `STP-04`.**

#### 04 — Preview before commitment

The key product moment is not moving a page. It is understanding the result before export. Preview connects editing to the final document, giving a person one more opportunity to check their work before it leaves the workspace.

**Editor — not public:** Show the original organisation, preview, and export screens in sequence. Explain actual state continuity, not imagined interactions.

**Insert `STP-05` and `STP-06`.**

#### 05 — Four days changes the design strategy

A short take-home rewards prioritisation. The important question is not how many features can appear in four days, but which relationships need to become clear. For this workspace, the page, its selection, its destination, and its final output form the core of that story.

**Editor — not public:** Add one real prioritisation decision from the assignment. Do not invent an alternative that was never considered.

**Insert `STP-07`.**

#### 06 — The useful next version

The next version should be tested around three questions: can someone identify what is selected, can they avoid a costly mistake, and can they predict what will be exported? These are proposed evaluation questions, not completed research findings.

**Insert `STP-08`, then the next-project handoff.**

## Project 3 — Dream Holidays

### Story angle

**Project label:** Dream Holidays  
**Proposed headline:** The holiday starts before the booking.  
**Proposed deck:** Reframing a service-led travel website as an editorial companion—helping people discover the shape of a trip, understand the details, and begin a useful conversation with an advisor.

### Proposed page narrative

#### 01 — A trip is more than a package card

Travel begins as a possibility. People imagine places, pace, and the kind of days they want before they are ready to compare final details. Dream Holidays needed a digital experience that could support that discovery while preserving the human guidance at the centre of the service.

The goal was not to make the advisor disappear. It was to make the first conversation better.

**Insert `DH-01`.**

#### 02 — A travel companion, not a checkout

A self-serve booking engine was the obvious digital pattern—and the wrong fit for this service. The chosen direction behaves more like an editorial travel companion: it helps a visitor understand a destination and arrive at the inquiry with context. The experience should support the service, not replace the part that makes it personal.

**Editor — not public:** Use the documented comparison between booking and an editorial companion. Do not add unsupported business constraints.

**Insert `DH-02` and `DH-03`.**

#### 03 — Let the itinerary do the storytelling

An itinerary should feel like a journey, not a database entry. Photography creates the possibility; hierarchy makes it understandable; practical detail helps someone judge whether the trip fits. The day-by-day structure is where those jobs meet.

**Editor — not public:** Use the strongest destination and itinerary screens, large enough to read.

> Enough detail to picture the trip. Enough space to keep imagining it.

**Insert `DH-04` and `DH-05`.**

#### 04 — Practical questions first, personal guidance next

The inquiry is the handoff between digital discovery and a human conversation. Its job is to bring useful context into that conversation and set expectations for what happens next. The final click should feel like a beginning, not a dead end.

**Editor — not public:** Show the actual questions and confirmation state. Do not claim increased lead quality without inquiry data.

**Insert `DH-06`.**

#### 05 — Different journeys, one calm structure

Destinations change. The reading rhythm should not. A visitor needs a recognisable structure for exploring the trip, checking the details, and continuing the conversation—even when the content or screen size changes.

**Editor — not public:** Select representative desktop and mobile screens that demonstrate the actual system.

**Insert `DH-07`.**

#### 06 — What the website can prove today

The direction gives digital discovery a clear role in a human-led service. The next question is whether people reach the inquiry with better context. Inquiry completion, the usefulness of submitted details, and advisor feedback would be useful ways to evaluate that—not numbers to assume in advance.

**Editor — not public:** Add observed results only where documented. Do not describe the journey as live or shipped unless verified.

**Insert `DH-08`, then the next-project handoff.**

## Project 4 — 6s SEO Tool

### Story angle

**Project label:** 6s SEO Tool  
**Proposed headline:** The numbers are here. Make the next move clearer.  
**Proposed deck:** A product-design case study about organising keyword and ranking information into a workflow that helps marketers move from overview to prioritisation and client-ready context.

### Proposed page narrative

#### 01 — Report-hopping is not a strategy

SEO work can generate plenty of information. The design problem is helping a marketer understand where to look first, compare the right context, and carry a useful explanation into the next conversation. This project focuses on the structure around those decisions.

The useful unit is not another metric. It is a decision the marketer can explain.

**Insert `SEO-01`.**

#### 02 — The overview should open a door

An overview is useful when it guides inspection. Its job is to establish the right scope, surface a relevant signal, and give someone a clear route into the detail. More information is not automatically a better starting point.

**Editor — not public:** Annotate only client, domain, keyword, or ranking categories visible in the actual screen.

**Insert `SEO-02` and `SEO-03`.**

#### 03 — A number without context is homework

A ranking value becomes useful when the surrounding view helps someone understand relevance, movement, priority, or the next investigation. The detailed view should answer the question raised by the overview instead of forcing the user to reconstruct it.

**Editor — not public:** Use the strongest detail screen. Call the feature “Opportunity Matrix” only if that name belongs to the design.

> The interface should not finish the marketer's thinking. It should give that thinking a clearer place to start.

**Insert `SEO-04`.**

#### 04 — Filters should narrow the view, not wipe the slate

Narrowing a dataset should not mean losing the original question. A person should be able to understand the scope of the view, recognise the active filters, and return to the wider picture. The challenge is keeping that continuity clear while the information gets more specific.

**Editor — not public:** Use a real filter/detail sequence. Static intended interactions must not be presented as a working prototype.

**Insert `SEO-05` and `SEO-06`.**

#### 05 — From working view to client conversation

Analysis does not end when the screen makes sense to its daily user. The next challenge is explaining the same information to someone with less context. A useful client conversation needs the observation, its significance, and a clear next discussion—not a larger pile of charts.

**Editor — not public:** Demonstrate this only with actual client-ready artifacts. Otherwise label it as a future workflow; do not claim manual reporting was replaced.

**Insert `SEO-07`.**

#### 06 — Outcome without theatre

The work aims to connect overview, investigation, and communication into one understandable story. The next evaluation would measure time to identify a priority, comprehension of a ranking change, and preparation effort. Those are targets for evaluation, not achieved results.

**Editor — not public:** Replace this proposed-plan ending with verified findings only when the original evidence supports them.

**Insert `SEO-08`, then the next-project handoff.**

## Page-level component rules

Build one reusable case-study system, then let project data control the story.

- `ProjectHero`: label, headline, deck, real hero/placeholder, status, role, verified timeframe, and scope.
- `ProjectContents`: project-specific chapter labels; no forced “Research / UX / UI / Result” taxonomy.
- `StoryChapter`: eyebrow, editorial title, short copy, optional pull line, and one primary media group.
- `ProjectFigure`: renders the real asset or the purposeful placeholder from the same media record.
- `MediaSequence`: two to four related screens with one shared caption; do not render six unreadable thumbnails.
- `DecisionCard`: choice, reason, cost, and unknown. Use only for decisions grounded in evidence.
- `EvidenceNote`: visible label for `Verified`, `Design artifact`, `Proposed`, or `Not yet measured` when ambiguity matters.
- `ProjectOutro`: honest outcome/reflection plus deterministic next project.

Do not create four large duplicate JSX files if the existing repository can express the pages as project data and composable sections. Do not force every project into identical chapter counts or identical visual cadence.
